Figure 5.6: Model Polymer

"The links of size \(b\) are connected to each other by freely rotating hinges. The contour length \(L\) is the length of the polymer if it were completely straight. In this case, if there are \(n\) links, \(L = nb\). The second length is the end-to-end length \(R\), which is the length from one end of the polymer to the other, and depends on the polymer configuration."

1. Microstates vs. Macrostates

In statistical mechanics, a microstate is an exact, microscopic description of every hinge orientation in the polymer chain. For a two-dimensional square lattice with \(n\) links, each link can point in 4 orthogonal directions (\(\rightarrow, \uparrow, \leftarrow, \downarrow\)), creating a total of:

\(\Omega_{total} = 4^n \quad \text{microstates}\)

A macrostate is defined by macroscopic observables, most notably the end-to-end vector \(\vec{R} = (X, Y)\) and its magnitude \(R = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}\).

2. Density of States \(\Omega(R)\) & Boltzmann Entropy

The density of states \(\Omega(R)\) is the number of distinct microstates that result in an end-to-end length of \(R\). In the microcanonical ensemble (where all microstates are equally likely), the probability of finding the polymer with end-to-end length \(R\) is:

\(P(R) = \frac{\Omega(R)}{\Omega_{total}}\)

According to Boltzmann's principle, the entropy \(S\) of a macroscopic state with \(\Omega(R)\) microstates is:

\(S(R) = k_B \ln \Omega(R)\)

The fully extended state (\(R = L = nb\)) has only 4 microstates (all links right, all up, all left, or all down). Its entropy is minimal (\(S = k_B \ln 4\)). In contrast, coiled conformations with \(R \approx \sqrt{n}b\) have exponentially more microstates, maximizing entropy.

3. The Mean Square End-to-End Distance

For an unrestricted ideal random walk of \(n\) steps of length \(b\), the mean square end-to-end distance obeys the fundamental scaling relation:

\(\langle R^2 \rangle = n b^2 \implies R_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle R^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{n}\,b\)

Notice that while contour length scales linearly with \(n\) (\(L \propto n\)), the typical coil dimension grows only as \(\sqrt{n}\). Therefore, long polymers in solution occupy a much more compact coil than their fully stretched length!

4. Entropic Elasticity & Biological Mechanics

Because thermodynamics drives systems toward higher entropy, a polymer pulled into an extended state experiences an entropic restoring force attempting to restore its coiled, high-entropy conformation:

\(f = -T \frac{\partial S}{\partial R} = \left(\frac{2 k_B T}{n b^2}\right) R = k_{entropic} R\)

This entropic elasticity explains the mechanical resilience of cellular structures:

  • Titin: The giant sarcomeric protein whose Ig domain unfolding and entropic coiling provides passive elasticity to cardiac and skeletal muscle.
  • Spectrin Network: The triangular spectrin spring network of red blood cell membranes providing extreme deformability through microcapillaries.
  • Actin Filaments & Microtubules: Semiflexible polymers governed by persistence length \(L_p\), where thermal bending undulations produce entropic tension.
